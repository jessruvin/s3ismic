Principle No. 2

Culture is infrastructure.

Every business has a culture, whether anyone wrote it down or not. It decides how the work actually gets done, and it only holds when it's built into the systems people use every day.

By Ruvin & Warrior 3 minute read Filed under: Culture, Systems

Anyone who has worked in IT knows the best compliment infrastructure ever gets is silence. Nobody rings to say the Wi-Fi worked today. They ring when it doesn't.

Culture works the same way. It's the unwritten rules: who actually makes the call, what counts as finished, whether the junior person mentions the problem in the meeting or after it. Every business has these rules long before it has a process document.

It shapes the work

Give two firms the same Microsoft 365 licences on the same day. In one, someone has set up a shared Teams channel by lunchtime and people are using it. In the other, files stay on desktops and get emailed round as v3_FINAL_final, because nobody wants to be the first to move. The software was identical. The difference was there before it arrived.

It shapes taste and style

Culture is also where taste lives. It's the person who sends a proposal back because the fonts don't match, and a team that thanks them for it. Now that anyone can produce a decent-looking document in thirty seconds, that instinct for what's actually good is getting rarer, and much harder to copy.

But it has to be built in

Trouble is, culture that lives only in values statements and away days doesn't hold under pressure. If the system rewards speed over care, care loses, however often the values say otherwise.

So it has to go into the plumbing: how work is briefed, who reviews it, what the tools make easy, where knowledge is kept and whether anyone can find it.

What it looks like

Mostly small things. A sign-off step that asks “is this good?” as well as “is this done?”. A SharePoint structure built around how the team actually files things, so people use it. A new starter's first week that covers how we write and review, alongside where the logins live. The process written down before the one person who knows it hands in their notice.

You don't need a culture programme for any of it. You'll know it's working when nobody mentions it, which, as anyone in IT will tell you, is the whole point.

Culture is infrastructure. Build it like infrastructure.

How we step in

Interceding.

/ in·ter·ced·ing / verb

To step in on someone's behalf. How we go into a business, what we do while we're there, and why leaving well matters most.

By Ruvin & Warrior 2 minute read Filed under: Culture, How we work

Most consultancies arrive with the answer already written. They spend a fortnight interviewing people, hand over a sixty-page report and leave. The report usually ends up in a SharePoint folder nobody opens again.

We'd rather intercede: step in alongside the people doing the work, stay long enough to understand it properly, make the change with them, then hand it back.

There are three stages. Skip one and it usually shows within six months.

01 Survey

We start by sitting in. The Monday meeting, the shared inbox, the spreadsheet everyone pretends isn't holding the business together. We talk to the people doing the work as well as the people managing it, and note where things keep getting stuck.

Mostly we're looking for the gap between how a business says it works and how it actually works. The org chart says approvals go to the finance director. In practice they go to the office manager, because she answers. Once we've found the gaps, we know what to change and what to leave alone.

02 Shift

Habits change first, then the tools. Rolling out a CRM to a team that keeps its real pipeline in a notebook changes nothing. So we start small, with the people who will live with the result.

The team makes the decision and the system gets built around it. We try each step with a handful of people before everyone depends on it, and there's always a way back. Change that gets imposed tends to get quietly worked around.

03 Hand over

This is the bit most people skip. Every system gets a named owner inside the business, documentation written for whoever joins next year, and admin access that sits with you. If your team can't explain what we built, or change it without calling us, we haven't finished.

Then we step back. You can always call. You just shouldn't need to.

Interceding is meant to be temporary. What counts is what's still standing after we've gone.

The words we work by

Proleptic.

/ pro·lep·tic / adjective

Anticipating what comes next, and acting before it arrives. Why the most useful thing a business can do right now is look up from the noise.

By Ruvin & Warrior 3 minute read Filed under: Culture, Technology

Proleptic comes from the Greek prolepsis, a taking in advance. In a story it's the flash forward. In an argument it's answering the objection before anyone raises it. We like it because it names a habit most businesses never have time for: seeing what's coming and acting before it arrives.

The pressure to move is constant. Copilot, ChatGPT and a dozen AI note-takers have all landed in the last couple of years. Five days in the office is now a negotiation. The graduate you hired last month expects to message the MD on Teams, and the MD still prints his emails.

Most businesses respond in one of two ways. Some wait and hope it blows over. Others buy whatever LinkedIn is excited about this month, then discover six months later that half the licences have never been opened. Both feel like decisions. Neither involved much deciding.

Looking up

Being proleptic means stopping long enough to ask which of these changes actually matter to your business, and which are just loud. Then moving early, in small steps, while it's still cheap to get things wrong.

Nobody knows what the next five years hold, and anyone who says they do is usually selling something. The aim is a business that can take a knock without falling over.

Grounded in values

Looking ahead only helps if you know what you won't change. The businesses that cope best are very clear on a few fixed points, usually how they treat clients and their own people, and relaxed about almost everything else.

So that's where we start. Before we talk about tools, we want to know what the business is for and where it's heading. The technology gets picked to fit. And the business keeps hold of it: its own admin accounts, its own data, and no contract it can't get out of.

Quiet work

Most of it is unglamorous. Asking the team what eats their week, and listening to the answer. Trying a tool with five people for a month before buying it for fifty. Agreeing in writing what you will and won't use AI for, before a client asks. Getting what's in the operations manager's head somewhere the rest of the business can find it.

It adds up. A year on, the business that did this work is the one that can move when the ground does.

The ground is always moving. The trick is seeing it before you feel it.

The words we work by

Et.

/ et /עֵת noun

Hebrew has two words for time. One for the minutes, and one for the moment that matters.

By Jess 2 minute read Filed under: Origins, Timing

We've all had that feeling... the one when you think of someone you haven't spoken to and then suddenly their name lights up your phone. You decide to change things up and end up meeting someone who changes the course of your week, sometimes your year.

Most people look at these as one-off coincidences, but I've always been a bit suspicious of how often they turn out to be the start of something.

Carl Jung had a word that describes this perfectly: synchronicity. Two things happen together with nothing causing one from the other, and yet they mean something.

That's where timing comes in.

Zman and et

Hebrew has two words for time. Zman is clock time, it's minutes. Et is the right moment... when two things that have been running side by side for years finally line up and suddenly it means everything and more.

Most of us spend most of our lives in zman, busy, working hard, getting on with it. Et doesn't care about any of that, it just turns up when it's ready, usually when you're least expecting it.

Out of nowhere

Some of the people who matter most to me came into my life that way, completely out of nowhere. What should have been a quick chat turned into a year, and then into something nobody could have planned.

I don't think anyone really warns you how much of life is just waiting... having the same conversations, coming back to the same ideas, feeling like something is about to change without any proof that it will. And if you're lucky, there's someone else feeling exactly the same thing at the same time.

A joining of happenings

Which brings me to S3ISMIC.

In Hebrew a coincidence is tzeruf mikrim, a joining of happenings, accidentally. Because that's what the good ones feel like... things that were always heading the same way, finally joining up at the right moment.

That's how S3ISMIC happened. Two people, conversations that began and never stopped, and moments that felt synchronous.

Here we are, noticing the small shifts before they become big ones.

Jess

Studio note

Blinkers on.

There are two ways to look at a market. One sees a fixed amount to fight over. The other sees something to make. We chose the second.

By Ruvin & Warrior 2 minute read Filed under: Ideas, Culture, Change

Most of the IT world runs on scarcity. There are only so many clients, and somebody is always about to undercut you on price per seat. So you watch the rivals, match their offers and shave your margin until everyone sells the same thing for slightly less.

It's tiring, and it makes your ambition exactly as big as the pie.

The creative world taught us the other way to look at it. A good studio doesn't spend its time working out how to win a bigger slice of existing work. It asks what nobody has made yet, and makes that.

Scarcity looks sideways

Spend your energy watching competitors and you end up copying them. Look at ten IT providers' websites: the same stock photo of a server room, the same promise of “peace of mind”. Nobody chose that. It's what happens when everyone looks sideways.

Creation looks ahead

A creative business spends its energy on what it can actually shape: how its own work gets done, the people doing it, and the standard it holds. It still knows what's going on around it. It just doesn't let that set the agenda.

Blinkers on

That's what we mean by blinkers on. A racehorse in blinkers can still hear the crowd. It just isn't looking at the other horses.

It's also why we don't spend much time thinking about competitors. Every client gets work made for them, so there's no template to copy and nothing for us to defend.

Abundance is practical

This is as practical as it is philosophical. Money spent training your team is still paying off in three years. Money spent matching a rival's discount is gone by next quarter.

And it changes how a team feels at work. People who are building something bring more to it than people who are holding on.

Fight for a share and there is never enough. Create, and there is always more to make.
